# 03 · What Gets Tested Before Publishing

*2026-08-23*

---

The Seam Stack makes a specific claim: that the schema governance component it provides — the part that determines when a field can be renamed, when a type can change, when an old version of a crossing record can be silently superseded and when it can't — occupies a space that, from everything swept to date, nothing else in the local-first ecosystem occupies. Before publishing that claim, adversarial testing ran against it from independent directions. Here's what happened.

---

First, a bit of housekeeping and level-setting: the schema governance problem explored here has lineage. The [Tiered Content Framework](https://www.jediwright.com/content-strategy-framework) specifies how content is structured, classified, and made legible across systems — including what it means for a vocabulary term to carry its governance context when it travels. It says nothing about how the vocabulary itself may change, who has the authority to change it, or what a downstream party should do when they can't verify that the change was governed. That's the layer above. That's what this entry is about.

---

The Seam Stack is built around a specific problem: when a local-first system crosses a boundary — a hiring event, a payment, a legal handoff, a clinical intake — the moment of crossing has to be legible later to parties who weren't there and can't check back in. That legibility requirement shapes the layers above the storage layer. It is why the boundary layer exists as a distinct surface, not a convention. It is why the evidence layer is not optional. And it is why the framework includes a schema governance component: a set of rules for managing how the vocabulary at the seam can evolve without breaking the records it already anchored.

The original form of the claim was something like: nothing else in the local-first ecosystem provides a governed decision layer for schema changes at this kind of boundary. That was a claim about an unoccupied space.

It needed to be tested.

---

The first sweep went through the local-first and CRDT ecosystem directly — the systems this work builds on — covering Cambria, Automerge's own documentation on the problem, Evolu (which markets schema evolution as a core feature), cr-sqlite, and [Seph Gentle's "Stable Schemas, Mutable Software" (aka schemaboi)](https://youtu.be/kWN8Q-jw6TY) from Local-First Conf Berlin 2026. Cambria, the bidirectional schema-lens project from Ink & Switch, is the most developed prior attempt at the problem adjacent to this one. It addresses how to translate between schema versions at read time — entirely at the mechanics layer: how do you read a document written under an older schema, after the schema changed? It doesn't say when a change is admissible, what evidence the person making the change should demonstrate before making it, or who has to live with the consequences if they get it wrong. The Automerge team, in a 2023 blog post, listed schema changes explicitly as an unsolved problem in local-first software. The schemaboi talk addressed the same translation problem Cambria does, from a different angle. Evolu and cr-sqlite, swept for any governance layer beyond compatibility checking, came back empty on the decision surface. What the sweep didn't find — and what it's honest to name explicitly — is whether systems like Yjs, DXOS/ECHO, or the broader event-sourcing schema-versioning literature occupy the same layer. The sweep was bounded; those territories weren't covered.

Within the territory covered, nothing was sitting in the decision layer. The space above the mechanics, where admissibility criteria, blast-radius accounting, and readability commitments would live, was unoccupied in the swept record.

The second sweep went through adjacent governance literature — standards bodies, versioning specifications, API lifecycle frameworks. It named FHIR, the healthcare interoperability standard, as the most developed analog it found. Before going further with that comparison: schema registries like Confluent's sit closer to this problem than most of the literature does. They enforce compatibility classes — backward, forward, full — with an automated check that rejects inadmissible changes at registration. That's decision-layer occupancy. It's institution-shaped (the registry is a coordinating service), and it operates on data stream schemas rather than boundary-crossing records. But the function is recognizably the same, and someone working with Kafka tooling will recognize it immediately. FHIR has a more developed analog still — a sophisticated process for when a field can be deprecated, what evidence is required before a breaking change is allowed, and how implementers are notified. It has exactly the kind of decision-layer thinking the local-first ecosystem lacks.

And it doesn't transfer, for a specific reason.

FHIR is a coordination instrument. The process works because HL7 exists: a body with the authority to mandate, with implementers who are reachable for feedback, with a committee that reviews the evidence and makes the call. FHIR does publish machine-readable, versioned artifacts — StructureDefinitions and implementation guides — and a party can validate a record against a pinned version offline without contacting anyone. What cannot be verified from the record alone is governance-process status: was this deprecation properly evidenced? Was process followed? That is what the institution holds. If you need to verify that a record was governed — not just that it conforms to a pinned spec version, but that the governance commitment behind it is still active — you rely on the institution. Healthcare records are litigated by adversarial, late-arriving parties all the time; FHIR's ecosystem solves that via archived published artifacts, which is a real solution. It's a design choice, not a gap. But it's a different design choice than the one the local-first seam requires.

The local-first seam has a different structure. The parties who need to verify a record later may be adversarial. They may be unreachable. They may arrive years after the fact, with no back-channel to any institution, in the context of a dispute. The commitment has to be readable from the record alone, without contacting anyone.

FHIR's decision heuristics transfer without HL7. The insight that different change classes carry different blast radii, that evidence should precede promotion, that there are maturity levels a change should pass through before being declared stable — all of that is sound and portable. What doesn't transfer is verification of governance-process status from the record alone.

---

At this point, the original claim was already narrower. The sweep hadn't found occupancy in the covered territory, but it had found what the claim was actually about. The decision layer wasn't unoccupied wholesale — the declaration and recording surface (version fields, changelogs, deprecation markers, supersession notes) is standard practice across everything swept. What was unoccupied in the covered territory was a specific composition: horizon-bounded readability commitments that a downstream party can verify from the record alone, combined with a check that fails closed on unconfirmed state.

Then an independent reviewer pressure-tested the framing and identified a territory that hadn't been swept: vocabulary and ontology governance. The framework's own worked example involves a versioned vocabulary. The closest analogs to what the framework specifies might live in the tradition that governs vocabularies and ontologies for a living.

So that got swept too.

---

Dublin Core, the metadata vocabulary standard, has something that most vocabulary governance doesn't: a threshold rule keying semantic changes to new URI assignment. By policy, if a change to a term's meaning is judged likely to have substantial impact on machine processing or functional semantics, a new term with a new URI must be created rather than updating the existing one. That is exactly the kind of blast-radius-classified change management the framework formalizes — editorial corrections leave the URI unchanged; semantically impactful changes require a new identifier. The rule is enforced by DCMI Directorate judgment, and the decisions are publicly documented with recorded rationale.

The OBO Foundry, a coordinating body for biomedical ontologies, has the richest structured deprecation practice in the field. OBO Principle 19 mandates a single governing rule for when a term must be deprecated rather than modified: if a proposed change to the definition would substantially change its referents — the real-world things the term denotes — a new term with a new IRI and definition must be created instead. Deprecation carries structured metadata: a controlled vocabulary of obsolescence reasons (including terms merged, placeholder removed, failed exploratory term, term split, and out of scope), replacement pointers, and links to the change discussion. Automated conformance checks run against every ontology the Foundry covers.

Neither tradition specifies what the framework specifies. The DCMI threshold rule is decided by the DCMI Directorate and reviewed by the Usage Board — and those decisions are publicly documented with recorded rationale. The evidence path exists. What it isn't is machine-verifiable from the term record itself: the documentation is institution-hosted and human-readable, which is a real difference from a commitment embedded in the record a counterparty holds. The retention commitment is indefinite: terms are maintained because DCMI recognizes that people depend on them, not because a policy-confirmed horizon has been declared with a dated expiration and a two-state record of whether it's been renewed. The OBO Foundry's deprecation checks are producer-side — they verify that the maintainer followed the rules, not that a reader's reference to a term is still under active governance. An obsolete term remains resolvable. That's a deliberate design decision, and the right one for OBO's domain: historical annotations need to remain interpretable, and failing closed on an obsolete term would destroy the record's usefulness. The design choice is correct for OBO. It's also not the design the local-first seam requires.

The pattern is the same as in FHIR and the schema registries, recurring within the vocabulary tradition: decision heuristics held by a coordinating body, highly developed, genuinely useful, and dependent on the institution to function at the verification step. The heuristics transfer. The commitment-and-verification composition — self-binding, counterparty-executable, institution-optional at verification time — does not appear in the covered record.

---

One clarification is worth making here, because the framework's prototype involves FHIR in a different capacity. In the prototype, FHIR appears as a wire format: a data shape for a crossing record that is published to a personal data store. That is a plumbing choice. The point above is about FHIR as a governance process — the HL7 standards body's decision architecture for when a healthcare data element can change. These are different things, and the closest analog finding is about the governance process, not the wire format.

---

Here is what the claim looks like now, after all of this:

For systems without a coordinating authority, the Seam Stack specifies a composition not found in the covered record: readability commitments bounded to a declared horizon, with a two-state confirmation record distinguishing "observed" from "confirmed active"; per-class change authorization coupled to evidence, keyed to blast radius; and a fail-closed governed-read check with named failure states that a downstream party can run from the record alone.

That last property depends on the record being tamper-evident — on the downstream party verifying something the authorizer cannot quietly change. In the prototype, that anchor is the AT Protocol PDS: crossing records are published to an append-only, externally hosted personal data store that the schema maintainer doesn't control. The verification runs against that record, not against whatever the maintainer currently says the record is. Without that external anchor, "verify from the record alone" would verify only the authorizer's current description of its own conduct. The external anchor is what makes the composition coherent.

It's worth being precise about what kind of dependency that anchor introduces. The composition does not eliminate trust requirements; it relocates them. Institutional governance systems like HL7 or DCMI require trust in an authority's discretion — membership, standing, and governed process are prerequisites for the verification path to function. The AT Protocol PDS requires trust of a different kind: that an append-only infrastructure layer behaves according to its protocol specification. These are materially different. Infrastructure trust is auditable against a public protocol; it doesn't require membership or standing; and it doesn't involve discretionary judgment about whether governance was properly conducted. A party verifying a crossing record doesn't need to know who HL7 is. They need to know that the external store hasn't been rewritten — a property that is checkable from the protocol layer, not from an institution's records. The dependency is real. It is not the same dependency.

The declaration and recording surface — version fields, changelog discipline, deprecation markers — overlaps established practice across everything swept. FHIR's decision heuristics transfer without an institution. Dublin Core's threshold rule and OBO's obsoletion rules are the same pattern in the vocabulary tradition: transferable heuristics, institution-dependent at the verification step. What the covered record doesn't show is the commitment-and-verification composition operating without a coordinating body present at the time of verification, anchored in a record that the authorizer doesn't control.

The Seam Stack relocates change authority rather than eliminating it. There is still an authorizer — the person or team that maintains the schema and makes and records the commitments. What's different is the structure: a commitment instrument in which the change authority binds its future conduct toward readers it will never coordinate with, with the binding detectable by a deferred party running a check against an external record. This is not enforcement in the strong sense — nothing mechanically prevents the maintainer from abandoning the schema or failing to renew a horizon. What the composition provides is detection: an ungoverned state produces a safe default rather than a misread. The deferred party knows what they're working with. The authorizer's defection becomes legible rather than silent. Detection is not recourse. A deferred party holding a clean error state still holds uninterpretable data, and the framework provides no mechanical path from that state to resolution. What it does provide is the precondition for recourse — a record whose governed status is auditable rather than opaque, from which a dispute can proceed. That is a different kind of authority from the institution's mandate; it is also a different kind of constraint from the native convention — a version field, an upgrade function, a note in the pull request — which structurally fails the deferred-party problem by producing no signal at all.

The adoptability claim narrowed too. The Seam Stack is not claimed to be ready for every local-first developer. It is adoptable, in principle, for builders of seams that carry legal or evidentiary weight — contexts where a deferred, possibly adversarial party needs to verify governed-read status without a back-channel, and where a misread carries real cost. That population is defined by the problem, not independently established. The narrowing process and the population arrived together: wherever the problem isn't present, the composition isn't needed. For the contexts where it is present, the covered record shows no alternative.

---

Publishing the shrinkage feels unusual. The more familiar pattern is to arrive with the broad claim already polished and the evidence organized to support it. The narrowing that produced the defensible form — the attacks that landed, the surfaces that turned out to be occupied, the analogs that turned out to be closer than expected — tends to stay in working documents.

What the narrowed claim still needs is contact with territory the sweeps didn't reach. The claim is scoped to the schema-governance application; the commitment-and-verification composition itself appears in supply-chain and security infrastructure — The Update Framework (TUF) is the sharpest known analog — governing software update metadata rather than schema vocabulary. A separate adversarial pass after publication also named two further unswept territories: W3C Verifiable Credentials and their associated status list specifications, which use cryptographically signed, time-bounded validity assertions published to append-only registries; and decentralized attestation systems like the Ethereum Attestation Service, which enforce schema-governed attestations with client-verifiable expiration states embedded in the record. Both address counterparty-verifiable commitments without central coordination — the same structural property the composition relies on. Whether either occupies the schema-governance application specifically, rather than the credential or attestation layer, is unknown. Whether anything in any of these territories occupies the specific application is unknown. The falsifier for what survived the sweeps is straightforward: one prior system exhibiting the composition in the schema-governance application would break it. If that system exists, it exists in unswept territory.

Whether the claim holds there is what further work is for.
